# Talk track off a profile

The line on the page gets someone to ask about it. This is what gets said next, in a recruiter screen, an interview, a demo, or a conversation about the repo.

Return speech. The test for every line is whether the person could say it out loud, verbatim, without translating it first.

## Contract

```
Main point
<one answer-first sentence>

Say this
- <spoken sentence>
- <spoken sentence>
- <spoken sentence>

Proof
- <the supplied fact behind each point, kept separate from interpretation>

Pushback
- "<the likely challenge>" -> <one direct response>

Close
<what you want to happen next>
```

Lead with "Say this" when the question is "what do I actually say". Explanation comes after the lines, if at all.

## Shape the answer

Answer first, reasons grouped under it, evidence under the reason it supports. Use a short situation and complication setup only where the listener genuinely needs it before the answer lands. Never as a wind-up.

**STAR has a place and it is narrow.** A behavioural interview question ("tell me about a time when") expects situation, task, action, result, and interviewers are often scoring against that shape. Use it there. Everywhere else, including "walk me through your resume" and "what does this project do", answer first and fill in behind.

## Time boxes

**30 seconds.** The main point, one proof, the close. Around 75 words. This is the recruiter screen answer and the "tell me about yourself" opener.

**2 minutes.** Main point, two or three spoken lines with proof under each, one named trade-off or limitation, the close. Around 300 words. This is the standard interview answer.

**5 minutes.** Add the second example, the pushback you expect, and what you would do differently. Only when they explicitly ask for depth.

Nobody complains that an answer was too short.

## Evidence discipline

This is where a talk track fails the person using it, because an invented number survives right up until someone asks where it came from, and that moment happens in the room.

- Prove only what the person supplied. Never invent a metric, a stakeholder reaction, a competitor move, a user quote, or a motive.
- A metric is evidence. "It improved adoption" is a claim and needs evidence of its own.
- Label the inference. "We did not measure that" and "that is my read rather than something we instrumented" are both usable lines, and both survive the room better than a number that does not hold.
- Name the trade-off. An answer with no cost in it gets heard as a sales pitch and treated like one.
- Do not use confidence or polish to cover a missing basis.

## Pushback

Four moves, in order.

1. **Take the question as asked.** Repeat it back in one clause so they know you heard the real version rather than a convenient one.
2. **Answer the strongest form of it.** Answering a weaker version is the most visible way to lose a room.
3. **Give the fact.** One, not four.
4. **Say what would change your mind**, or what you would need to know. That is what makes the position sound held rather than performed.

Never argue with an objection nobody raised.

## The most common questions off these documents

**"Walk me through your resume."** Chronology is a trap; it takes four minutes and lands nothing. Give the through-line instead: where you started, the one pivot that explains the shape, where you are now, and why this role is the next step. 90 seconds.

**"Tell me about this number."** Say what it measured, over what period, against what baseline, and what you personally did. If it was a team result, say so and say what your part was. Claiming a team result as your own is the failure mode interviewers are actively probing for.

**"Why did you leave?" / "Why the gap?"** One sentence, no defensiveness, then move to what you did with the time. Do not narrate the internal politics of a former employer.

**"What does this project do?"** One sentence on the problem, one on who it is for, one on the interesting technical decision. Then stop and let them pick where to go.

**"Why is there no test coverage on this repo?"** Say the real reason, say what you would do with more time, and do not pretend the gap is a design choice. An owned limitation reads as judgement. A defended one reads as blindness.

## Keep it speakable

- Full sentences that can be said without rewording.
- One idea per line, one breath per line.
- Ordinary words and contractions.
- "I recommend" and "my concern is" over abstract nouns.
- The person's uncertainty and ownership stay visible. Do not make them sound more certain than they are.
- No dashes in spoken lines. A dash is a reading mark; it tells the speaker nothing about where the breath goes.
- Skip "as we all know", "at the end of the day", "let me take you through", "at a high level".

## Boundaries

- Do not coach pressure tactics, false urgency, or anything that misrepresents the record.
- Do not script an answer that claims work the person did not do.
- If the honest answer to a likely question is "I do not know", say so and propose how they would find out. That is a better answer than a confident wrong one and interviewers know it.
- Compensation, offer negotiation and counter-offers sit outside this skill.
